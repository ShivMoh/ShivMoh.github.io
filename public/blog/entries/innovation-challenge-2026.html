The 2026 Innovation Challenge and SkillTree

Oct 9, 2026 6 min read

Thoughts and Reflections

Late random blog post, but I participated again in the 2026 Innovation Challenge hosted by the Office of the Prime Minister in collaboration with SBM Offshore. I had participated last year as part of FramePerfect Lite and won with our solution Minerva, but to be honest, I more or less followed directions that time. I didn't feel as though I was able to really help shape the solution into what it was, so while I did win, it didn't really feel like my win.

This year was different. I wasn't gonna be hard carried by experienced devs, and I was supposed to play the part of the team leader. Our team was ITech (abbreviated from Imataka with I, find out more here: ImatakaTech). Our team had four members: Reynard Etwaroo, Ezra Minty, Clarence Lucius, and myself.

Crafting the initial Idea

Any whos, back to the meat and potatoes. The premise this year was pretty vague, likely due to agentic coding being very good at this point. So the question posed to us was: "How might technology help Guyanese citizens acquire the skills needed for careers in the rapidly growing energy industry?"

At first, I kept thinking of how to go about addressing this. I was at work, so I spent the first day just brainstorming until I reached what was probably the obvious solution, that being, a recommender service. There was already tons of stuff out there in terms of actual content, such as CourseraXGoal and free tertiary education for Guyanese students. But still, I didn't think this was enough. And it was obvious. I remember that one of my previous teammates, Andrew, from FramePerfect Lite hinted at the same premise for the solution (we work together at GLSC). Soo, yh, it was the obvious direction to go in, but that didn't mean it was wrong. I did have a call with my teammates, and we decided to go along with it.

Anyways, fast forward, I get there, the team starts working, and we're out here cooking. Day one ended with us just getting the solution up. Reynard and I, both avid Obsidian users, both would've immediately jumped on the visualization angle of skills using graphs and showing that as a way to see where you stand in terms of development. Ezra was building out the service layer for actually taking in a resume and recommending skills. Clarence was tasked with doing foresight prediction for jobs and skills, something I told him to look into without thinking much, but it became important down the line. But the graphs were the thing that really sold the concept of our solution.

Differentiating the solution, Killing the Competition

I don't remember the exact sequence of events. This might've been on the first night or the second night. I'm not sure which. But I kept thinking of how to differentiate the solution. And I thought of how I could find a unique angle to poke at the problem statement. So I thought about my own life, and what I'm bad at and what has helped me... networking. I suck at networking, and I'm really bad, but it's also what helped me get certain jobs here in Guyana. Especially in Guyana, having the right links here is almost everything.

So I broke down how to go forward, and I was explaining it to Reynard. One problem of the premise was "How might technology help..." Guyanese acquire the skills they need for energy sector careers, but it was leading somewhat that getting the skills was enough to enter the energy sector. It is not. Having a connection and getting in front of the right people is the other, arguably more important, aspect. There are, in my opinion, a lot of people in the high-paying energy sector that probably aren't the best but still they are "good enough," and they knew the right people. So there's that.

Anyways, as to how we show that in our solution, simple really. LinkedIn integrations as a graph and event notifications, pulled straight from Facebook. And that's it really. The rest was just selling that aspect of it and visualizing the skills someone in the field has vs. what you have.

The other thing which our solution lacked... and which we had thought of before was VR/AR practical training solutions. I did notice one or two teams going along that direction, so it did worry me a little that this was something we could've done as well. But the obvious thing about this was that creating VR/AR simulations for practical training was not very scalable, and importantly, there were already solutions out there... so my answer to this was simple. Our system already simply analyzed your resume, pulled your skills, looked at the job you want, pulled skills from there, and then finally found the difference and recommended courses based on that. Courses at this point were being pulled from Coursera, University of Guyana, and more. But to explicitly compete with practical training simulations, we added some other platforms such as InteractPlay, which explicitly focused on courses along those lines. And we would make this a point in our pitch: we're the facilitator. This made our solution all-encompassing but also very scalable since we don't have to bother with creating the platforms ourselves.

The Pitch

Day 3, we were done mostly. We spent the early morning practicing our pitch. Our solution was crafted. And when the time was right, it was to just go out there. And we did, and we did good. And previous teammates, Shomari (also my previous supervisor at UG) and Andrew, came to see us present. And judges aside, they were impressed. At this point, we didn't know that we had won as yet, but that was a win in my book.

The rest of the day went by, the closing ceremony came by. They started announcing the winners.

Third place went to GStack (checks out).

Second place went to LudgeTech (strange but okay...).

And...

First place... ITech! (We won!)

And that was that. The end.

Thoughts and Reflections

I was happy that I won, but same as with last time, it was momentary. I'm glad my solution won. The biggest thing for me was that it proved my ideas had value. But I think when it comes to tech, competitions are easy. They're just playgrounds. Once you know the right things to say, and the right way to do things, it just becomes obvious how to go about it. It's not a guarantee, but it's a controlled environment. What I want next is to actually put something out there and try to get people to actually use it. I think that's the real test for whether something works or not. Soo, with that said, we're still developing Skill Tree. You can find it at skilltree.gy.
